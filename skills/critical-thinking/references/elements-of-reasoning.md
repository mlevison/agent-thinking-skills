# The Elements of Reasoning
Eight things present in every piece of reasoning. Each can be examined on its own, and each is a place reasoning goes wrong.

Offer one question at a time. Explain the element in a sentence. Wait for the answer before choosing the next one. The order below is a reference, not a route - see `entry-points.md`.

## Purpose
*All reasoning has a purpose.* If you can't say what you're trying to achieve, you can't tell whether you're getting there.

Purpose should finish the sentence "I'm doing this in order to ..."

- What are you actually trying to accomplish here? Say it in one sentence.
- Whose purpose is this - yours, your manager's, the team's?
- Is this purpose realistic, and is it worth what it will cost you?

**Fails like:** Going to the garage for a screwdriver and coming out three hours later having reorganised the shelves.

## Question at Issue
*All reasoning is an attempt to settle a question.* A vague question does not have a clear answer. Two people can read it and answer it differently.

- What exactly is the question you're trying to settle? Write it as a question.
- If you shared this question with a friend, would they understand clearly what you're looking for?
- Is this three questions wrapped in a trench coat? Separate them and pick the most important.
- Which kind of question is this - settled by evidence, by preference, or by judgement?

**Fails like:** An hour spent arguing about whether to get a dog, when one person means ever and the other means this year.

**Routing:** If it really is a fact question and an external source exists, say so and stop. Send them to check the source rather than working through the rest of this file. A metric answering a question that was never factual is the case to catch - "did the reorg work?" resolves to a number only once someone quietly picks a definition.

## Information
*All reasoning is based on data, information and evidence.* The risk is not that we didn't search for evidence, it's that we searched for evidence that supports our current thinking.

- What information and evidence do you already have? State the evidence and not the conclusions drawn from it.
- Where did it come from, and how would you find out if it were wrong?
- What evidence would count against your hypothesis? Have you looked for it as hard as you looked for support?
- What's missing? Distinguish data you haven't gathered from data that doesn't exist.
- Is this evidence directly relevant to the question you just stated, or to a nearby one?

**Fails like:** Asking GenAI about your symptoms until it agrees it's nothing serious.

## Inferences
*All reasoning contains inferences by which we draw conclusions.* People report their inferences as though they were observations, and stop being able to tell the difference.

- What conclusion have you drawn, and what specifically took you from the evidence to it?
- Where does the evidence stop and your interpretation start?
- Could the same evidence support a different conclusion? Provide one.
- Which single inference is carrying the most weight? What happens if it's wrong?
- Does the conclusion follow, or does it just feel like it fits?

**Fails like:** "They're annoyed with me." The evidence is that they haven't replied to a text since this morning.

## Concepts
*All reasoning is expressed through concepts and ideas.* Many stuck arguments are two people using one word for two things.

- What are the key terms here? Define the one you'd assume everyone already agrees on.
- Would two people in that meeting define it the same way? Have you checked, or assumed?
- Whose definition are you using? Does it serve them differently than it serves you?
- Is there a different concept that would frame this better? What does it make visible that yours hides?

**Fails like:** Two people planning a budget holiday, one picturing youth hostels around Europe and the other a deal on a hotel in Monaco.

## Assumptions
*All reasoning is based on assumptions.* These are the things you take for granted without noticing, which is why you cannot list them without being asked.

- What are you taking for granted here?
- What would have to be true for your reasoning to hold?
- Which assumption, if false, collapses the whole position?
- Where did that assumption come from: evidence, habit, or the culture you're immersed in?
- What are you assuming about how people will behave? Assumptions about human behaviour are usually weakest.

**Fails like:** Opening the meeting with "so you've all read the proposal" about an email sent an hour ago.

## Point of View
*All reasoning is done from some point of view.* It is a frame of reference that shows you some things and hides others.

- From what perspective are you seeing this? What would someone with a different view see?
- Who is affected by this and not in the conversation?
- Who would disagree? What is the strongest version of their case? One that they would recognise as fair.
- What does your role make easy to see, and what does it make hard?
- What would you be arguing if your incentives were reversed?

**Fails like:** A tall person deciding how high to hang the shelves.

## Implications
*All reasoning leads somewhere.* Implications follow whether or not you intended them, and the ones you dislike are the ones you skip.

- If you're right, what follows? Provide three, including one you would be unhappy with.
- If you're wrong, what does that cost? Who pays the price?
- What happens in six months? In two years? Second-order effects show up late.
- What's the implication you're hoping nobody raises?
- What would you have to undo if you changed your mind later, and can you?

**Fails like:** Getting the puppy without working out who walks it in February.
