# The Elements of Reasoning
Eight things present in every piece of reasoning. Each can be examined on its own, and each is a place reasoning goes wrong.

Offer one question at a time. Explain the element in a sentence. Wait for the answer before choosing the next one. The order below is a reference, not a route. See `entry-points.md`.

## Purpose
*All reasoning has a purpose.* Until it is stated, there is no way to tell whether the work is getting there.

Purpose should finish the sentence "I'm doing this in order to ..."

- What is this meant to accomplish? Say it in one sentence.
- Whose purpose is this: yours, your manager's, the team's?
- Is the purpose realistic, and is it worth what it will cost?

**Fails like:** Going to the garage for a screwdriver and coming out three hours later having reorganised the shelves.

## Question at Issue
*All reasoning is an attempt to settle a question.* A vague question does not have a clear answer. Two people can read it and answer it differently.

- What exactly is being settled here? Write it as a question.
- Would someone else read that question and know what counts as an answer?
- Is this three questions wrapped in a trench coat? Separate them and pick the most important.
- Which kind of question is this: settled by evidence, by preference, or by judgement?

**Fails like:** An hour spent arguing about whether to get a dog, when one person means ever and the other means this year.

**Routing:** If this is a fact question and an external source exists, say so and stop. Send them to check the source rather than working through the rest of this file. The case to catch is a metric answering a question that was never factual: "did the reorg work?" resolves to a number only once someone quietly picks a definition.

## Information
*All reasoning is based on data, information and evidence.* The risk is not a failure to search. It is searching where support was likely to turn up.

- What information and evidence are already in hand? State the evidence, not the conclusions drawn from it.
- Where did it come from, and what would show it to be wrong?
- What evidence would count against the hypothesis, and where would it be found?
- What is missing? Distinguish data nobody has gathered from data that does not exist.
- Is this evidence relevant to the question just stated, or to a nearby one?

**Fails like:** Asking GenAI about your symptoms until it agrees it's nothing serious.

## Inferences
*All reasoning contains inferences by which we draw conclusions.* They get reported as though they were observations, and the difference stops being visible.

- What conclusion has been drawn, and what connects the evidence to it?
- Where does the evidence stop and interpretation start?
- Could the same evidence support a different conclusion? Provide one.
- Which inferences are carrying the most weight? What happens if they turn out to be wrong?
- Does the conclusion follow, or does it fit a familiar pattern?

**Fails like:** "They're annoyed with me." The evidence is that they haven't replied to a text since this morning.

## Concepts
*All reasoning is expressed through concepts and ideas.* Many stuck arguments are two people using one word for two things.

- What are the key terms here? Define the one everyone is assumed to agree on.
- Would two people here define it the same way?
- Whose definition is this, and does it serve them better than it serves everyone else?
- Is there a different concept that would frame this better? What does it make visible that this one hides?

**Fails like:** Two people planning a budget holiday, one picturing youth hostels around Europe and the other a deal on a hotel in Monaco.

## Assumptions
*All reasoning is based on assumptions.* These are the things taken for granted and never noticed, which is why they go unlisted until someone asks.

- What is being taken for granted here?
- What would have to be true for the reasoning to hold?
- Which assumptions, if false, collapse the position? There may be one, there may be several.
- Where did each assumption come from: evidence, habit, or the surrounding culture?
- What is assumed about how people will behave? Assumptions about human behaviour are usually the weakest.

**Fails like:** Opening the meeting with "so you've all read the proposal" about an email sent an hour ago.

## Point of View
*All reasoning is done from some point of view.* It is a frame of reference that shows some things and hides others.

- From what perspective is this being seen? What would someone with a different view see?
- Who is affected by this and not in the conversation?
- Who would disagree? What is the strongest version of their case, one they would recognise as fair?
- What does your role make easy to see, and what does it make hard?
- What would the argument be if the incentives were reversed?

**Fails like:** A tall person deciding how high to hang the shelves.

## Implications
*All reasoning leads somewhere.* Implications follow whether or not they were intended, and the unwelcome ones are the easiest to skip.

- If this is right, what follows? Provide three, including one that would be unwelcome.
- If this is wrong, what does it cost, and who pays?
- What happens in six months? In two years? Second-order effects show up late.
- Which implication would be the least comfortable to raise?
- What would have to be undone after a change of mind, and can it be?

**Fails like:** Getting the puppy without working out who walks it in February.
